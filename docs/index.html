Effective Hamiltonians for generic ice models

A Python engine that generates terms of the Rayleigh–Schrödinger series for the effective Hamiltonian \(H_{\mathrm{eff}}\) of quantum spin ice, with fully symbolic couplings — ready for spatial disorder.

Overview

Ice models -- water ice, spin ice, the checkerboard antiferromagnet, and indeed Kagome ice -- are an intuitive recipe for emergent gauge theories in classical and quantum spin liquids.

This repo defines a 'generalised ice model' as a special case of the constrainer Hamiltonian \[ H_0 = \sum_{t} J_t \mathcal{C}_{t}^2,\hspace{4em} \mathcal{C}_{t}=\sum_{j\in t} \eta_{j,t}S^z_j\] where the sum over \(t\) runs over all of the constraints, the coupling constants \(J_t>0 \forall t\), and the scalars \(\eta_{j,t} = \pm 1\). In fact, we require slightly more — the \(\eta_{j,t}\)'s must be interpretable as the incidence numbers of a chain complex. Stated otherwise, there must be a globally consistent way to understand \(\mathcal{C}_t\) as a lattice divergence \(\mathcal{C}_t=\nabla\cdot\,S^z~.\)

In traditional pyrochlore spin ice, these 'constraints' are the tetrahedra, and the \(\eta_{j,t}\) are +1 if \(t\) is pointing 'up', -1 if \(t\) points 'down'. Though the sign factor makes no difference at the level of the Hamiltonian, this conveniton allows the \(\mathcal{C}_t\)'s to be understood as conserved charges counting the spinon number.

This project implements the “general procedure for generating terms” from the PhD thesis (Sanders 2026, Ch. 1 and Appendix B). The goal is a general tool: given any off-diagonal perturbation on any ice lattice, it symbolically integrates out all violations of the ice manifold to produce a series of symbolic effective operators up to arbitrary order in \(V\), which act within the ice manifold.

Cell complexes for children

This section is a very slight refactoring of standard simplicial homology theory — the construction here differs only in that the elementary cells are not necessarily (hyper)triangular [Massey 1991]. The familiar reader will recognise this as a special case of singular homology in CW complexes. The technical points of difference between these formalisms are not important to the present discussion.

To begin with, we will define the abstract notion of a lattice. We require more structure than a simple periodic array of points; rather, we will think of a hierarchy of oriented, primitive, non-decomposable objects

\[ \{\mathrm{Volumes}\} \supset \{\mathrm{Plaquettes}\} \supset \{\mathrm{Links}\} \supset \{\mathrm{Vertices}\}. \]

We call an elementary \(r\)-dimensional object an \(r\)-cell, and write \(\mathcal{K}_{r}\) for the set of elementary, oriented objects. The elementary voids, plaquettes, links and vertices of a lattice are the 3-, 2-, 1-, and 0-cells respectively.

The boundary of an \(r\)-cell is a formal sum of several lower-dimensional hulls traversed in a particular order (see the figure below). We therefore introduce the notion of an \(r\)-chain:

Definition (\(r\)-chain). An \(r\)-chain \(c_r\) is a formal, \(\mathbb{Z}\)-valued linear combination of \(r\)-cells \[ c_r = \sum_{\sigma_r \in \mathcal{K}_r} c_r(\sigma_r)\,\sigma_r. \] We call the set of all possible \(r\)-chains \(C_r\).

In situations where orientation is unimportant, we write \(\sigma \in c_r\) to mean that the \(r\)-cell \(\sigma\) has a nonzero coefficient in the chain \(c_r \in C_r\).

The set \(\mathcal{K}_r\) plays the role of a basis for the \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module \(C_r\). In this finite-dimensional setting, there is an obvious isomorphism in which the \(r\)-chain \(c_r\) can be thought of as an \(r\)-cochain \(c^r \in C^r\), i.e. a map

\[ \begin{aligned} c^r : \mathcal{K}_r &\to \mathbb{Z}\\ \sigma &\mapsto \text{coefficient of }\sigma\text{ in }c_r. \end{aligned} \]

We will therefore refer to the two as essentially synonymous, and use the notations \(X(r)\) and \(X_r\) interchangeably for cochains.

We now aim to implement vector calculus on the chain complex. The final piece of structure comes from the boundary operator \(\partial : C_r \to C_{r-1}\); these being chosen such that for any given cell \(\sigma_r \in \mathcal{K}_r\), its boundary \(\partial(\sigma_r)\) may be thought of as an \((r-1)\)-chain.

Definition (cell complex). A finite-dimensional cell chain complex is a collection of sets \[ C_n \xrightarrow{\partial} C_{n-1} \xrightarrow{\partial} \cdots \xrightarrow{\partial} C_0 \] with maps \(\partial_n : C_n \to C_{n-1}\) such that
  1. (finite-dimensional) there exists a maximum cell dimension \(n\in\mathbb{Z}\), and
  2. (exact) \(\partial^{2} = 0\).

Subtly, all of the orientation information in the complex is specified by the explicit form of the maps \(\partial\). For practical calculations, it is convenient to express \(\partial\) as a matrix with respect to the bases \(\{\mathcal{K}_{r-1}, \mathcal{K}_{r}\}\). Let \(\tau_{r-1} \in \mathcal{K}_{r-1},\ \sigma_{r}\in\mathcal{K}_{r}\) be elementary cells, and define the incidence numbers

\[ \iota_{\tau_{r-1}\sigma_{r}} = \begin{cases} 1 & \tau_{r-1} \subset \sigma_{r},\ \text{correct orientation}\\ -1 & \tau_{r-1} \subset \sigma_{r},\ \text{wrong orientation}\\ 0 & \text{else} \end{cases} \]

which we use to define the \(\mathbb{Z}\)-linear operator \(\partial\),

\[ \partial c^{r}(\tau_{r-1}) = \sum_{\sigma_r \in \mathcal{K}_r} c^r(\sigma_r)\,\iota_{\tau_{r-1} \sigma_r}. \]
Diamond chain complex.

For the diamond lattice, setting up the complex consists mainly of choosing orientation conventions for all of the constituent \(r\)-cells and then specifying \(\partial\) such that it respects a right-hand rule. The standard choice shown in the figure above reads as follows:

  • 0-cells, i.e. the centres of tetrahedra, have no orientation.
  • 1-cells \(\sigma_1 \in \mathcal{K}_1\) point from \(\Up\) tetrahedra to \(\Down\).
  • 2-cell normal vectors point from \(\Up\) to \(\Down\) on the dual lattice.
  • 3-cells always take the outward normal.

Notice that the orientation convention for the 2-cells is intimately tied to the 1-cells: if inversion symmetry is broken by making \(\Up\) and \(\Down\) tetrahedra (0-cells) distinguishable, then the corresponding voids (3-cells) necessarily become distinguishable.

Lattice operators

We now generalise the notion of an \(r\)-(co)chain to include operators on a Hilbert space.

Definition (lattice field). Let \(R\) be a ring. An \(R\)-valued \(r\)-(lattice) field is a ring homomorphism \[ F : C_r \to R. \] We write \(C^r(R)\) for the space of \(R\)-valued \(r\)-fields.

A lattice field can be uniquely specified by its action on all of the basis \(r\)-cells, via

\[ \begin{aligned} F: C_r &\to R \\ F(c_r) &= \sum_{\sigma\in\mathcal{K}_r} c^r(\sigma)\,F(\sigma). \end{aligned} \]

Note that a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-valued \(r\)-lattice field is simply an \(r\)-cochain.

Definition. The boundary operator \(\partial\) acts on a lattice \(r\)-field \(F\) to produce the \((r-1)\)-field \(\partial F\), \[ \partial F(\tau_{r-1}) = \sum_{\sigma_{r}\in \mathcal{K}_{r}} \iota_{\tau_{r-1},\sigma_r}\,F(\sigma_{r}). \]

Dual to \(\partial\) is the coboundary operator \(\delta\), \(\delta_n: C_{n-1} \to C_{n}\). With respect to a particular choice of basis \(\mathcal{K}_r\), it is defined as

\[ \delta F(\sigma_{r+1}) = \sum_{\tau_{r} \in \mathcal{K}_{r}} \iota_{\tau_{r},\sigma_{r+1}}\,F(\tau_{r}). \]

This definition is made such that, given \(c_r\in C_r\) and \(b_{r-1} \in C_{r-1}\), \([\partial c_{r}](b_{r-1}) = c_r(\delta b_{r-1}).\) Thought of as large, rectangular matrices, the coboundary \(\delta\) is simply the transpose of the boundary.

The dual lattice

Starting with an abstract chain complex \(C_r\) there is a canonical way to formally construct a dual complex \(\bar{C}_r\), in which the \(r\)-chains of the original lattice become the \((d-r)\)-chains of the dual.

Formally defining a chain complex is equivalent to specifying all incidence numbers. We construct these based on the principle that for a \(d\)-dimensional complex, \(\bar{c}_{r} \simeq c_{d-r}\),

\[ \iota_{\bar{\sigma}_{r}\bar{\sigma}_{r+1}} = \iota_{\sigma_{d-r-1},\sigma_{d-r}}. \]

A given \(r\)-chain \(F \in C_r\) may be mapped to \(\bar{C}_r\) by simply defining \(\star F(\bar{c}_r) \deq F(c_{d-r})\). One may then straightforwardly derive the identities

\[ \begin{aligned} \partial \star F(\bar{c}_r) &= \delta F(c_{d-r}),\ \text{and} \\ \delta \star F(\bar{c}_r) &= \partial F(c_{d-r}), \end{aligned} \]

where the equality above is as members of \(X\), the target space. Thus we recover the famous \(\delta = \star \partial \star\) from differential geometry. The dual therefore contains the same connectivity information, with the roles of \(\partial\) and \(\delta\) reversed. One may also read immediately that all objects are invariant under \(\star \star\), i.e. the 'dual dual' lattice is simply the original lattice.

From this very abstract discussion, it is not immediately obvious that the dual lattice can be embedded 'naturally' in ordinary space. For the special case of the diamond lattice, it so happens that the dual lattice is isomorphic to the original lattice — the diamond plaquette centroids are themselves distributed on a pyrochlore lattice. This is not the case on e.g. the honeycomb, which is dual to the triangle lattice and vice versa.

Integration and Stokes’ theorem

Having introduced the necessary machinery, we now come to the most important result — the discretised lattice Stokes’ theorem. The lattice equivalent of integrating over a submanifold \(\Omega\) is summation: if \(F\) is an \(r\)-field, then

\[ \sum_{c_r \in \Omega_r} F(c_r) \equiv \sum_{\sigma_r \in \mathcal{K}_r} c_{\sigma_r}\, F(c_{\sigma_r}) \]

represents a sum over the elementary cells, weighted by the coefficient of \(c_r\) in \(\Omega_r\), which implements the concept of orientation.

Theorem (lattice Stokes’ theorem). Let \(\Omega_r\) be an \(r\)-chain, and \(F : C_{r-1} \to R\) a generic lattice field. Then \[ \sum_{\sigma_{r-1} \in \partial \Omega_r} F(\sigma_{r-1}) = \sum_{\sigma_{r} \in \Omega_r} \delta F(\sigma_r). \] A similar identity holds when \(\partial\) and \(\delta\) are exchanged, \[ \sum_{\sigma_{r+1} \in \delta \Omega_r} F(\sigma_{r+1}) = \sum_{\sigma_r \in \Omega_r} \partial F(\sigma_r). \]
Proof. One need simply choose a basis, and then expand definitions: \[ \begin{aligned} \sum_{\sigma_{r-1} \in \partial \Omega_r} F(\sigma_{r-1}) &\equiv F(\partial \Omega_r), \\ = \sum_{\sigma_{r-1} \in \mathcal{K}_{r-1}} \sum_{\tau_r \in \mathcal{K}_{r}} \iota_{\sigma_{r-1} \tau_r}\, \Omega_r(\tau_{r})\, F(\sigma_{r-1}) &\equiv \delta F(\Omega_r) = \sum_{\sigma_{r} \in \Omega_r} \delta F(\sigma_r). \qquad\blacksquare \end{aligned} \]

Chain reformulation of the ice problem

Consider a real-valued 1-field \(E\in C^1(\mathbb{R})\), representing an electric field in standard Maxwell electromagnetism in 3+1D, and the \(\mathbb{R}\)-valued 0-field \(\phi\) for the scalar potential. The definition

\[ E \deq -\delta \phi = -\star \partial \star \phi \]

is sufficient to immediately and unambiguously translate \(E = -d\phi\) from the usual non-relativistic formulation of classical electromagnetism, in which the base manifold is \(\mathbb{R}^3\), \(E\) and the vector potential \(A\) are 1-forms, and the magnetic field \(B\) is a 2-form formally independent of \(E\) [Frankel 2011]. Likewise, the magnetic field takes the obvious form \(B = \delta A\).

Introduce the operator-valued monopole charge 0-field \(Q\), which can be expressed in terms of the 1-field \(S^z\) as

\[ Q = \partial S^z. \]

Comparing with the original definition on the diamond lattice, we see that the role of \(\eta_t\) is now absorbed into the orientation convention. This construction exhibits the geometric origin of monopole charge conservation without appealing to any particular lattice structure. In general, there is no guarantee that \(Q\) has any particular physical meaning — for this, we must postulate a form for the Hamiltonian:

Definition (general ice problem). A generic, classical ice model on the cell complex \(\mathcal{K}_n\) places a spin on each link \(l \in \mathcal{K}_1\). These spins interact under the Hamiltonian \[ H = \sum_{t \in \mathcal{K}_0} \frac{J_\parallel(t)}{2}\bigl(\partial S^z(t)\bigr)^2. \] The \(J_\parallel(t)\) are real, positive, possibly \(t\)-dependent constants. Stated otherwise, \(J_\parallel \in C^0(\mathbb{R})\).

This can be recognised as a special case of the general constrainer Hamiltonian for classical spin liquids introduced by [Yan et al. 2023]. For now, we will limit the discussion to the case that each 0-cell has an even number of bonds (in the spin language, each corner-sharing polyhedron has an even number of spins), which renders \(Q\) integer-valued and so ensures that the ground-state charge configuration is unique. The half-integer case introduces additional complexity, which will be duly discussed in Chapter 4. Notice that, after a suitable local change of basis, an arbitrary Ising chain can be formulated as an ice model as in the figure above (panel b), with the spinons identified as domain walls.

Now consider the operators which create spinon–antispinon pairs. These operators act by flipping spins along a path, generating excitations at the endpoints.

Definition (string operator). Let a collection of spin-\(S\) spins lie on the links (1-cells) of a lattice, with their \(S^z\) axes thought of as oriented in the same way as the basis links \(l \in \Links\). Given a 1-chain \(\gamma \in C_1\), define the string operator \[ \mathcal{O}(\gamma) = \prod_{l \in \gamma} \frac{S^{l}}{\tilde{s}}, \] where \[ S^{l} = \begin{cases} [S^{+}]^{|\gamma(l)|} & \gamma(l) > 0\\ [S^{-}]^{|\gamma(l)|} & \gamma(l) < 0\\ 1 & \gamma(l)=0, \end{cases} \] and \(\tilde{s} \deq S+\tfrac{1}{2}\).

These operators act non-trivially only on the spins along the support of \(\gamma\). When applied to an ice state — i.e. a state satisfying \(\partial S^z(t) \ket{\psi} = 0\ \forall t\) — they generically introduce Gauss-law violations at the endpoints of \(\gamma\). To make this precise, we consider how \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma)\) interacts with the charge operator.

Lemma (charge transport by strings). Let \(\gamma \in C_1\) be a string. Making use of \([S^z(\sigma), S^{l(\sigma)}] = l(\sigma)\,S^{l(\sigma)}\), we derive the identity \[ \begin{aligned} [\partial S^z(t), \mathcal{O}(\gamma)] &= \sum_{l'} \iota_{t l'}\, [S^z_{l'}, S^{\gamma(l')}] \prod_{l \neq l'} S^{\gamma(l)}_l \\ &= (\partial \gamma)(t)\, \mathcal{O}(\gamma). \end{aligned} \] It follows immediately that string operators preserve all charges if and only if \(\partial \gamma = 0\), i.e. they are loops.

Though \(\mathcal{O}\) resembles an exponential map, i.e. a group homomorphism between 1-chains under addition and spin operators under multiplication, it is not quite so simple. The non-invertibility of the spin ladder operators introduces certain caveats. If one attempts to add 1-chain \(\gamma\) to \(\eta\), then \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma + \eta)\) generally only factorises into \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma)\mathcal{O}(\eta)\) when \(\gamma\) and \(\eta\) are disjoint. Nonetheless, we can derive a precise identity —

Lemma (cancellation of lines). Let \(S=\tfrac{1}{2}\). Then, so long as both \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma)\) and \(\mathcal{O}(\eta)\) are nonzero, \[ \mathcal{O}(\gamma)\, \mathcal{O}(\eta) = \mathcal{O}(\gamma + \eta) \prod_{l \in \gamma\cap \eta} \left[\tfrac{1}{2} + \gamma(l)\, S^z_l \right]. \]
Proof. For each link \(l \in \mathcal{K}_1\), there are only two cases to consider: (i) \(\gamma(l) = 0\) or \(\eta(l)=0\), and so one of the operators is trivial, or (ii) \(\gamma(l) \neq 0\) and \(\eta(l) \neq 0\). Supposing that \(\gamma(l) = \pm1\) and \(\eta(l) = \mp1\), the identity \(S^\pm S^\mp = \tfrac{1}{2} \mp S^z\) provides the required form. Otherwise, if \(\gamma(l)+\eta(l) \neq 0\), then \(\mathcal{O}(\eta + \gamma)\) necessarily annihilates all states and so both sides of the equation vanish. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

This lemma illustrates that string operators 'interfere' with one another. In particular, when two strings partially overlap with opposite orientations, they either restore the spin to its original eigenvalue or annihilate the state entirely. This makes the space of string operators non-Abelian under naïve multiplication, even in the absence of any background gauge field.

The picture simplifies in the semiclassical limit. If \(|S^z|/S\) is small, then we have \(S^+S^- \simeq S(S+1) + O\!\left(S\,\tfrac{S^z}{S}\right)\); in other words a constant as \(S\to \infty\). In this limit, all of the string operators commute with one another, revealing \(\mathcal{O}\) to be the Wilson line of an Abelian lattice gauge theory. In this case \(\mathcal{O}\) becomes a homomorphism between the spin algebra (viewed as a \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module under operator multiplication) and the \(\mathbb{Z}\)-module of 1-chains. If \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are elementary plaquettes, then \(\mathcal{O}(\partial p_1)\mathcal{O}(\partial p_2) = \mathcal{O}(\partial(p_1 + p_2))\) — in other words, \(\mathcal{O}\) behaves like a Wilson loop.

In the realistic case \(S=\tfrac{1}{2}\), however, the string vanishes if any coefficient in \(\gamma\) exceeds \(|\gamma(l)| > 1\). From this it is easy to see that oriented paths on the lattice (i.e. 1-chains made of only \(c_l \in \{-1,0,1\}\)) are in one-to-one correspondence with the string operators \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma)\).

Interestingly, \(\mathcal{O}\) retains its Abelian character even at \(S=\tfrac{1}{2}\) if the loops are suitably arranged. Suppose \(p_1\) and \(p_2\) are adjacent elementary plaquettes sharing exactly one link \(l'\), where \(l'\) is traversed in opposite directions on \(\partial p_1\) and \(\partial p_2\). The symmetrised product satisfies

\[ \begin{aligned} \mathcal{O}(\partial p_1)\mathcal{O}(\partial p_2) + \mathcal{O}(\partial p_2)\mathcal{O}(\partial p_1) &= \mathcal{O}(\partial[p_1 + p_2]) \left[ \tfrac{1}{2}\pm S^z_{l'} + \tfrac{1}{2}\mp S^z_{l'} \right] \\ &= \mathcal{O}(\partial[p_1 + p_2]), \end{aligned} \]

revealing that we can at least in some sense construct extended Wilson loops by chaining plaquettes. We will return to this notion of symmetrised loops later.

The loop operators, being in some sense conjugate to the charges, are essential to understanding the degeneracy of the ground-state manifold. In the classical theory of [Yan et al. 2023], they are exactly the fluctuators.

Theorem. Let \(\mathcal{M}\) denote the set of ice states satisfying \(Q_t=0\,\forall t \in \mathcal{K}_0\). Any two ice states \(\ket{\psi}, \ket{\phi} \in \mathcal{M}\) may be connected by flipping closed loops of spins.
Proof. Take two classical ice states \(\ket{\psi}, \ket{\phi} \in \mathcal{M}\). For any \(S\), the difference of \(S^z\) eigenvalues between the states \[ \gamma(l) = \bra{\psi} S^z_l \ket{\psi} - \bra{\phi} S^z_l \ket{\phi} \] defines an integer-valued 1-chain. One can then verify that \(\mathcal{O}(\gamma) \ket{\phi}\) yields \(\ket{\psi}\) up to normalisation. Since \(Q_t\) is the same on all \(t\in \mathcal{K}_0\), \([Q_t, \mathcal{O}(\gamma)] = 0\) for all \(t\in \mathcal{K}_0\). The charge-transport lemma above then shows \(\partial \gamma = 0\). \(\gamma\) is therefore a (possibly disconnected) closed loop of spins. \(\qquad\blacksquare\)

It follows that applying all possible loop operators to an arbitrary seed state \(\ket{\psi_0}\) generates the entire ice manifold \(\mathcal{M}\). If the cohomology group

\[ H^1 = \{\text{closed loops}\} \,/\, \{\text{loops that are boundaries of plaquettes}\} \]

is nontrivial, as is generally the case in periodic boundary conditions, then the elementary plaquette-flip operators are not enough to generate the full Hilbert space from a given seed state. This subtlety will play an important role later.

Perturbative formalism

Start with the Brillouin-Wigner formalism. Split the Hamiltonian into a diagonal part and a small off-diagonal perturbation, \(H = H_0 + V\). For QSI,

\[ H_0 = \sum_{t}\frac{J_\parallel(t)}{2}\bigl(\partial S^z(t)\bigr)^2 , \qquad V = \text{off-diagonal (spinon-transport) terms}. \]

\(H_0\) counts the magnetic “charge” \(\partial S^z(t)=\eta_t \sum_{i\in t}S^z_i\) on every tetrahedron \(t\); the ice manifold \(\mathcal M\) is its degenerate ground space (all \(\partial S^z(t)=0\)). With \(P\) the projector onto \(\mathcal M\), \(Q = 1-P\), and \(G_0(E) = [E-H_0]^{-1}\), the Brillouin–Wigner effective Hamiltonian is

\[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{\mathrm{BW}}(E) - H_0 = P\bigl[\,V + V G_0(E) Q V + V G_0(E) Q V G_0(E) Q V + \cdots\bigr]P . \]

Expanding both \(H_0\) energy dependence on \(V\) gives the Rayleigh–Schrödinger–Kato series,

\[ H_{\mathrm{eff}}^{(n)} = \!\!\sum_{\substack{m\ \text{factors of}\ V\\ m+\sum a = n}}\!\! V Q G_0^{(a_1)} V Q G_0^{(a_2)} \cdots V , \]

whose leading, “irreducible” piece is term \(V\,(Q G_0(E_0) V)^{n-1}\), identical to the BW expression evaluated at \(E=E_0\).

String operators and the linked-cluster theorem

The special form of an ice model -- a constrainer model having only

If all constraints can be simultaneously set to zero -- as is the case in clean spin ice -- then we immediately see that all loops must be closed. If, however, any three-member or one-member constraints are present, then the ground state sees not \(C_t=0\), but a "zero point" \(C_t=\pm \tfrac12 \).

The generating procedure

  1. Fix an order \(N\) — this sets the number of string-operator “paths”.
  2. Arrange the paths into a connected, closed loop so that all gapped spinons are annihilated (linked-cluster).
  3. Write down the \(N!\) orderings of the chosen operator set; for each, compute the virtual spinon energy entering every propagator factor \([E-H_0]^{-1}\).
  4. Sum over all orderings.

For the breathing-pyrochlore XXZ model the leading non-trivial order is \(N=3\): three two-site hops close into a hexagon and produce the canonical ring exchange

\[ \Bigl[\,3!\,\tfrac{J_{\pm,B}^{3}}{J_{zz,A}^{2}} + 3!\,\tfrac{J_{\pm,A}^{3}}{J_{zz,B}^{2}}\Bigr]\, S_1^{+}S_2^{-}S_3^{+}S_4^{-}S_5^{+}S_6^{-} \;=\; g\,\mathcal O_p . \]

How the engine works

Because the effective operator is local, the engine reconstructs it from its action on the support of the process alone. For a given cluster and order \(N\):

  1. For every \(S^z\) pattern \(\sigma \in \{\pm 1\}^{\text{support}}\) (each a would-be ice state), it performs a depth-\(N\) search over the available string terms, evolving the configuration hop by hop.
  2. A branch is kept only if every intermediate state carries spinons (lies in \(\mathcal{M}^\perp\)), the final state returns every incident tetrahedron to zero charge (a closed loop), and the touched sites are connected on the interaction graph (linked-cluster).
  3. Each surviving ordering contributes \(\bigl(\textstyle\prod \text{couplings}\bigr)\cdot \prod_k \frac{-1}{E_k}\), where \(E_k\) is the virtual spinon energy after \(k\) hops. Since \(\sigma\) is (locally) an ice state, these energies depend only on the hop-induced charges and are therefore background-independent.
  4. Diagonal \(S^z\) factors in a term multiply the amplitude by their (configuration-dependent) eigenvalues.
  5. The accumulated amplitudes, as functions of \(\sigma\), are mapped back to operators by a Walsh/Fourier transform (using \(\sigma_i = 2 S^z_i\)): a diagonal polynomial in the \(S^z\) of the support, plus one ladder monomial per off-diagonal flip channel (with any \(S^z\) dressing on the spectator sites).
Symbolic by construction. Couplings and virtual energies are sympy expressions carrying per-sublattice (and, in general, per-bond) symbols. The noncommutative operator structure is represented with the Commutation package, which also renders LaTeX. Disorder is just “more symbols”.

Arbitrary off-diagonal terms

The engine consumes any list[StringTerm]. A StringTerm is a product of ladder operators (factors, each (site, ±1) for \(S^\pm\)) with an optional set of diagonal \(S^z\) factors (sz_sites) and a symbolic coupling — enough to express any off-diagonal spin term. The \(S^z\) factors contribute their configuration-dependent eigenvalues to the amplitude, so \(J_{z\pm}\)-type and decorated operators arise automatically.

Every channel of the generic QSI Hamiltonian is a one-liner:

from rsqsi.terms import StringTerm, hop, pmpm, field, zpm

hop(i, j, Jpm)                                  # S⁺_i S⁻_j
pmpm(i, j, Jpmpm)                               # S⁺_i S⁺_j
field(j, h)                                     # [S⁺_j, S⁻_j]   (h Sˣ_j)
zpm(n, o, +1, Jzpm)                             # Sᶻ_n S⁺_o
StringTerm(((a, +1), (c, -1)), g, sz_sites=(k,))  # g · Sᶻ_k S⁺_a S⁻_c   (+ .dagger())

Spread a local template across the lattice with the replicators place_on_bonds(model, factory) / place_on_sites(model, factory) (factories receive the model, so they can reach neighbours for dressings). For anything the replicators do not cover, build the StringTerms directly.

Example checks

Transverse field, \(N=2\)
\[ -\,\frac{2h^2}{J_{zz,A}+J_{zz,B}} \]

The site lies on one A and one B tetrahedron, so both \(J_{zz}\) enter the denominator.

\(S^z\)-dressed hop

A spectator \(S^z_k\) multiplies the plain-hop result by \((S^z_k)^2 = \tfrac14\) and introduces no \(S^z_k\) dependence — an exact spin-½ bookkeeping check the test suite asserts.

Minimal API example

import sympy as sp
from rsqsi.lattice_model import build_pyrochlore
from rsqsi.terms import xxz_perturbation
from rsqsi.bw import generate_bw

model = build_pyrochlore(L=2)          # 32-site breathing-pyrochlore supercell
V     = xxz_perturbation(model)        # Σ Jpm (S⁺S⁻ + h.c.), coupling per sublattice

hexagon = frozenset(model.rings[0].members)
res = generate_bw(model, V, N=3, support=hexagon)

print(sp.simplify(res.ring_coefficient()))
# 6*J_pm_A**3/J_zz_B**2 + 6*J_pm_B**3/J_zz_A**2
